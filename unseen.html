The Unseen and the Mysteries

الْغَيْب

Everything hidden, with its evidence in the open

Al-ghayb, the unseen, is what Allah kept out of sight and told us about anyway: the angels who are with you now, the jinn who were here first, the nations already judged, and the promises still ahead. Every claim in this room wears a badge that says exactly what stands under it, and where the sources stop, the room stops with them.

Jibril, the Trustworthy Spiritجِبْرِيلُ

The angel who carried every word of the Qur’an to the Prophet ﷺ was seen in his true form exactly twice.

The Qur’an names him directly: say, whoever is an enemy to Jibril, it is he who brought the revelation down upon your heart by Allah’s permission (Qur’an 2:97). Surah al-Shu’ara calls the same carrier al-Ruh al-Amin, the Trustworthy Spirit, who descended with it upon the heart of the Prophet ﷺ in a clear Arabic tongue (Qur’an 26:193-195). Jibril alayhis-salam is not a symbol or a literary device. He is the appointed messenger between Allah and His prophets, entrusted with the words on which everything else in this religion stands.

Surah al-Najm describes the one who taught the Prophet ﷺ as one of mighty power, who stood plainly on the highest horizon, then drew near (Qur’an 53:5-10). The mainstream position of the commentators, resting on the explanations of Aisha and Ibn Mas’ud in the Sahih collections, is that this figure is Jibril. Those same narrations state that the Prophet ﷺ saw him in the form in which he was created only twice, once filling the horizon and once at the Lote Tree of the furthest limit, and that he has six hundred wings (Bukhari 3232, Muslim 177, with Qur’an 53:13-18).

Mikail, Named Beside Jibrilمِيكَائِيلُ

Only one ayah names Mikail, and it makes enmity toward him enmity toward Allah.

Mikail alayhis-salam appears by name once in the Qur’an: whoever is an enemy to Allah, His angels, His messengers, Jibril, and Mikail, then Allah is an enemy to the disbelievers (Qur’an 2:98). The commentators relate that the ayah answered those who had declared Jibril their enemy, and its logic is sweeping: hostility toward any of Allah’s appointed envoys, angelic or human, is hostility toward Him. That a single verse sets Mikail beside Jibril in this way was enough for the scholars to count him among the greatest of the angels.

As for his work, the description found across the tafsir tradition is that Mikail is charged with the rain and the vegetation, the measured provision that keeps every living thing fed. That level should be stated honestly: it comes from reports of the early generations, for example under Qur’an 2:98 in Ibn Kathir, not from an ayah or a rigorously authenticated hadith that spells it out. What the strongest sources do secure is his rank: the Prophet ﷺ opened his night prayer with, O Allah, Lord of Jibril, Mikail, and Israfil, Creator of the heavens and the earth (Muslim 770).

Israfil and the Trumpetإِسْرَافِيلُ

The blast that ends the world fills the Qur’an, yet the name of its blower reaches us by a different road.

The Qur’an is explicit about the Sur, the Trumpet. It will be blown, and whoever is in the heavens and the earth will fall dead except whom Allah wills, then it will be blown again and all will be standing, looking on (Qur’an 39:68). Another ayah calls it a single blast (Qur’an 69:13), and a third shows mankind arriving in crowds on the Day it sounds (Qur’an 78:18). The event is certain and repeated across the revelation. What the Qur’an never does is name the angel whose task it is.

The name arrives through the Sunnah and the scholars. The Prophet ﷺ called on Allah as Lord of Jibril, Mikail, and Israfil (Muslim 770), and an authentic hadith describes the companion of the Horn already holding it, waiting for the command to blow (Tirmidhi 2431). That this waiting angel is Israfil alayhis-salam is how the reports were understood across the generations; al-Qurtubi and Ibn Hajar relay it as the well-known, settled position, and some transmit consensus on it. So the honest order is: the blast is Qur’an, the blower’s name is hadith and scholarly agreement.

The Angel of Deathمَلَكُ الْمَوْتِ

Revelation describes his task with perfect precision and never once gives him a name.

Say, the angel of death who has been entrusted with you will take you, then to your Lord you will be returned (Qur’an 32:11). One angel, singular, appointed over the ending of every life. Other ayat widen the frame: Allah sends guardians over each servant until, when death comes to one of you, Our messengers take him, and they never fail (Qur’an 6:61), and the angels, plural, are described taking souls (Qur’an 4:97). The commentators reconcile these readings simply: the angel of death has angel assistants, and no soul’s appointment is ever missed.

Then there is the name. Azrail fills stories, films, and even some classical pages, yet it occurs nowhere in the Qur’an and nowhere in any authentic hadith. Ibn Kathir states plainly that the angel of death is not named in the Qur’an or the sound narrations, and that the name Azrail comes only through some later reports, drawn largely from the lore of earlier communities (Ibn Kathir, al-Bidaya wa’l-Nihaya). Revelation calls him malak al-mawt, the angel of death, and a believer loses nothing by keeping exactly to the words revelation chose.

Malik, Keeper of the Fireمَالِكٌ

The Qur’an has already recorded the plea the people of the Fire will make to their keeper, and his answer.

The Qur’an preserves a conversation that has not happened yet. And they will call out: O Malik, let your Lord make an end of us. He will say: you are remaining (Qur’an 43:77). One name, one office, recorded before the event. Around him stand the rest of the Fire’s custodians, angels stern and severe who do not disobey Allah in what He commands them (Qur’an 66:6), nineteen set over Saqar, and none but angels appointed its keepers (Qur’an 74:30-31). Malik alayhis-salam is no mythology; he is an obedient servant carrying out a command he will never exceed.

The Sunnah confirms the name. In the long dream vision shown to the Prophet ﷺ, the one he saw kindling the Fire was identified to him as Malik, the keeper of Jahannam (Bukhari 7047). The Qur’an elsewhere records the people of the Fire pleading with its keepers, plural, to lighten one day of punishment, and being reminded that their messengers had already come with clear proofs (Qur’an 40:49-50, Qur’an 39:71). The scene in Surah al-Zukhruf then closes on Malik’s answer, quiet and final: you are staying.

The Keeper of Paradise and the Name Ridwanرِضْوَانُ

That Paradise has keepers is revelation; the famous name Ridwan is a later inheritance.

The Qur’an gives the scene. Those mindful of their Lord are led to Paradise in companies until, when they arrive and its gates are opened, its keepers say: peace be upon you, you have done well, so enter it to remain forever (Qur’an 39:73). Keepers, khazanah, welcoming rather than barring the way. The Sunnah adds a gate and a first opening: the Prophet ﷺ said that he will come to the gate of Paradise on the Day of Resurrection, and the keeper will ask who he is, and will say he was commanded to open for no one before him (Muslim 197).

The name Ridwan is a separate matter. It became the standard label for the chief keeper of Paradise in later books, sermons, and poetry, and reports carrying it were gathered in works such as al-Suyuti’s collection on the angels, but it is not found in the Qur’an, nor in the strongest hadith collections. The office is revealed, the greeting is revealed, the name is inherited from reports below that rank. Saying the keeper of Paradise costs nothing and stays inside the evidence, which is exactly how talk of the unseen should behave.

The Two Questioners of the Graveمُنْكَرٌ وَنَكِيرٌ

That two angels will question you in the grave is firmly established; their famous names travel on a narrower road.

The questioning itself stands on firm ground. The Prophet ﷺ described how, when a person is buried and his companions turn away, he hears their footsteps, and two angels come to him, sit him up, and ask him about his Lord, his religion, and the man who was sent among them (Bukhari 1338, Muslim 2870, and the long hadith graded sahih in Abu Dawud 4753). The Qur’an’s promise that Allah keeps firm those who believe with the firm word, in this life and in the Hereafter (Qur’an 14:27), was explained by the Prophet ﷺ as revealed about the grave (Bukhari 1369).

The names are narrower. A narration in al-Tirmidhi calls the two angels of the grave al-Munkar and al-Nakir (Tirmidhi 1071); al-Tirmidhi graded it hasan gharib, and later specialists split over it, some accepting the naming as hasan and using it in creed texts, others judging the naming weak while affirming the questioning itself without hesitation. So the careful sentence runs in two registers: two angels will question you, by texts the ummah agrees are authentic, and they may bear these names, by a report whose grade is honestly contested.

Harut and Marut, the Trial at Babylonهَارُوتُ وَمَارُوتُ

One ayah carries their entire story, and it is stricter and cleaner than the legends built on top of it.

Everything established about them sits inside one ayah, and it begins by clearing a prophet: Sulayman did not disbelieve, but the devils disbelieved, teaching people magic, and what was sent down upon the two angels at Babylon, Harut and Marut, who would teach no one until they had said: we are only a trial, so do not disbelieve (Qur’an 2:102). The ayah adds that people learned from them what separates a man from his wife, that no one is harmed by it except by Allah’s permission, and that whoever purchases it has no share in the Hereafter.

Around that ayah later storytelling wrapped a famous tale of two angels who lusted, drank, killed, and hang punished in Babylon until the Hour. Ibn Kathir surveys those reports and concludes that none of it is authentically traceable to the Prophet ﷺ, and that its substance goes back to the transmitted lore of earlier communities (Tafsir Ibn Kathir 2:102). The Qur’an’s own account needs none of it: angels acting only as a trial decreed by Allah, a warning issued before every lesson, and the guilt resting where the ayah leaves it, on the one who buys.

Kiraman Katibin, the Noble Scribesكِرَامًا كَاتِبِينَ

No word leaves your mouth without a noble scribe, already seated, writing it down.

Over you are keepers, noble, writing, who know whatever you do (Qur’an 82:10-12). The Qur’an describes the arrangement with courtroom precision: when the two receivers receive, seated on the right and on the left, a person utters no word without a watcher beside him, ready (Qur’an 50:17-18). The scribes are called kiram, honored ones, and the record they keep is not surveillance for its own sake; it is the evidence of an accounting in which nothing is lost, nothing misheard, and nothing entered against the wrong name.

The Sunnah shows mercy written into the rules of the ledger. The Prophet ﷺ related from his Lord that whoever intends a good deed and does not do it has a complete good deed written for him, and whoever does it has it written from ten times up to seven hundred and beyond, while an evil deed merely intended and left undone is written as a complete good deed, and an evil deed done is written as one (Bukhari 6491). The pen over your shoulder, in other words, is weighted toward forgiveness.

The Guardians in Successionالْحَفَظَةُ

Angels guard you in relays, before and behind, until the moment the written decree arrives.

For each person there are angels in succession, before him and behind him, guarding him by the command of Allah (Qur’an 13:11). The word is mu’aqqibat, ones who relieve one another in relays around a single human being. Another ayah completes the arc: He is the Subduer over His servants, and He sends guardians, hafazah, over you, until, when death comes to one of you, Our messengers take him, and they never fail (Qur’an 6:61). A life, in these two ayat, is escorted from beginning to end by appointed and unfailing custody.

The Prophet ﷺ described one such rotation: angels of the night and angels of the day take turns among you, gathering at the dawn prayer and the afternoon prayer, and those who spent the night among you ascend, and their Lord asks them, though He knows best, how they left His servants, and they answer: we left them praying (Bukhari 555, Muslim 632). Ibn Abbas and the early commentators added the sober edge: the guardians shield a person by Allah’s command, and when the decree itself arrives, they make way for it.

The One UFO Encounter in History With an Answer

One night in Madinah, unidentified lights over a reciter of the Qur’an received what no modern sighting has: an identification.

Usayd ibn Hudayr, may Allah be pleased with him, was reciting Surah al-Baqarah in the night, his horse tethered beside him, when the animal began to stir. He fell silent and it calmed; he resumed and it reared again. His son Yahya lay near the horse, so he stopped for the boy’s sake, looked up, and saw something like a canopy above him, filled with what looked like lamps, rising into the sky until he could no longer see it. In the morning he told the Prophet ﷺ, who told him: recite, Ibn Hudayr, recite, Ibn Hudayr (Bukhari 5018, Muslim 796).

Then came the identification. Those, the Prophet ﷺ said, were the angels drawing near for your voice, and had you kept reciting until morning, the people would have looked at them; they would not have been hidden from them. Strictly speaking, a UFO is only what its letters claim: a flying object unidentified at the moment it is seen. By that plain definition, Usayd saw one, lights aloft that he could not name that night. What removes this sighting from the modern catalog is that it did not stay unidentified; the answer came from the Prophet ﷺ himself.

The boundary matters as much as the wonder. This report identifies one night’s lights above one reciter in Madinah; it does not hand us a master key to every strange light since. Islam affirms unseen beings, angels and jinn, but no text teaches that modern aerial reports are those beings, and nothing here licenses that leap. What the account does teach is what drew the angels close: not spectacle, not summons, but the Book of Allah recited in the dark by a believer, so beautifully that the sky’s inhabitants came near to listen.

Created from Smokeless Fireوَخَلَقَ الْجَانَّ مِن مَّارِجٍ مِّن نَّارٍ

Before the first human breathed, another creation already walked the earth, made of something stranger than clay.

The Qur’an states plainly that the jinn were created from fire: ’He created the jann from a smokeless flame of fire’ (55:15), and elsewhere, ’the jann We created before, from the fire of scorching wind’ (15:27). Two facts stand on these ayat. First, their substance: marij, a smokeless, mixing flame, and samum, a burning wind. Second, their timing: min qablu, before Adam. The jinn are not metaphors or psychological forces in the Qur’an’s telling. They are a creation, deliberate and dated, older than us.

By default they are unseen: ’he sees you, he and his tribe, from where you see them not’ (7:27). Notice what the ayah grounds and what it does not. It grounds their reality and our blindness to them, not their omniscience or omnipresence. The believer holds both truths without drama: a parallel creation exists, and Allah has veiled it from our eyes in ordinary life. Fear is not the lesson here. Awareness is, and the ayah itself was revealed as a warning against Shaytan’s deception, not an invitation to obsess over the hidden.

Believers and Rebels Among Themوَأَنَّا مِنَّا الصَّالِحُونَ وَمِنَّا دُونَ ذَٰلِكَ

The Qur’an records a confession from the jinn themselves: some of us are righteous, and some of us are otherwise.

Surah al-Jinn opens with an event, not a legend: a company of jinn heard the Qur’an recited and said, ’indeed we have heard an amazing recitation, guiding to right conduct, so we believed in it’ (72:1-2). The same surah lets them describe their own society: ’among us are the righteous, and among us are otherwise, we are ways divided’ (72:11), and ’among us are those who submit, and among us are the unjust’ (72:14). Belief, doubt, obedience, rebellion: the full moral range we know in ourselves.

Their accountability rests on the same foundation as ours: ’I did not create the jinn and mankind except to worship Me’ (51:56). One purpose, two creations. This is why the surah ends with jinn facing the same outcomes we face, fuel for Jahannam for the unjust among them (72:15) and a right path for those who submit. The jinn are not props in human stories. They are a tested community, and the fair conclusion is neither fascination nor dread but recognition: unseen neighbours under the same Lord, sitting the same exam.

Iblis and Shaytan: the Name and the Roleإِبْلِيسُ وَالشَّيْطَانُ

One is a name, the other is a job description, and the Qur’an never confuses the two.

Iblis is an individual: ’they prostrated, except Iblis. He was of the jinn, and he rebelled against the command of his Lord’ (18:50). One being, one refusal, one story. Shaytan is broader. Surah an-Nas seeks refuge from the whisperer ’from among the jinn and mankind’ (114:6), and Allah speaks of ’shayatin of mankind and jinn’ inspiring each other with adorned speech (6:112). Shaytan names a function, the rebel who whispers and misleads, and both species can fill it.

Why does the distinction matter? Because 18:50 settles a real question: Iblis was of the jinn, not a fallen angel, since angels do not disobey (66:6). And because 114:6 protects us from a lazy assumption: that all our harm comes from the unseen. A human being can play the shaytan to another human. Keeping the name and the role separate keeps our theology clean and our vigilance honest, aimed at whispers wherever they come from, not at shadows.

The Qur’an mentions the ifrit exactly once, and what it actually says is more restrained than the stories.

Later literature built elaborate ranks of ifrit kings, armies and territories. None of that is in the ayah. So we affirm what is written, a class of jinn marked by strength, and we file the rest under storytelling. In Bukhari there is also a report of the Prophet ﷺ restraining an ifrit who tried to interrupt his prayer, showing the word lived beyond this one court.

One Qur’anic word for rebellion produced a thousand tales; here is the part that is actually revelation.

In Surah as-Saffat, Allah describes adorning the lower heaven with stars ’and a protection from every defiant shaytan’ (37:7). The word is marid, from a root carrying brazenness and persistence in rebellion. The same description recurs: people follow ’every rebellious shaytan’ (22:3), and idolaters call upon ’a defiant shaytan’ (4:117). So marid in revelation is an adjective of character, the hardened rebel among the shayatin, shut out from the highest assembly and repelled when it tries to listen (37:8-10).

Later imagination turned the adjective into a species: towering marids in bottles, wish-granting captives, sea giants. Those images belong to One Thousand and One Nights and its cousins, not to the Qur’an or the Sunnah. The honest position holds the line in both directions. Revelation affirms defiant, hardened rebels among the shayatin, and says nothing of ranks, bottles, or wishes. When a book, film, or folk tale presents the marid as a catalogued creature with rules, it is quoting literature, and literature should be enjoyed, or declined, under its own name.

Every person walks with an unseen companion, and the Prophet ﷺ did not exclude himself.

A lifetime of whispering, then a disowning. The practical lesson is neither paranoia nor denial. The qareen explains the pull we feel toward the low path, it does not excuse following it, and dhikr, wudu and the Qur’an starve its influence. Blame stays with the one who obeys the whisper.

The First War

The earth before us, in six chapters

Before the first human footprint, the earth was already inhabited. The Qur’an states it plainly: the jinn were created before mankind, from the fire of a scorching wind, and they dwelt upon the earth. What their age looked like, and how it ended, revelation leaves mostly in shadow; the early community did not. From Ibn Abbas and the students of the companions come reports of corruption, of blood, and of an army sent down, preserved by at-Tabari and Ibn Kathir. This room tells that story fully, and tells you, at every step, exactly how it reached us.

The anchors are revelation; the battle itself reaches us through the early community, and every chapter says which is which.

1The Earth Before UsQur’an-confirmed

The Qur’an opens this story with certainty. ‘And the jinn, We created before, from the fire of the scorching wind’ (15:27); ‘He created the jinn from a smokeless flame of fire’ (55:15). Before there was a mankind to remember them, there was a creation of fire, real, willed, and accountable, and the earth knew their footsteps before it knew ours. They are near yet unseeable by design: ‘he sees you, he and his tribe, from where you do not see them’ (7:27), words revealed about Iblis and his kind, describing an order of creatures woven into the world beyond the reach of our eyes. That the jinn existed before us, that they were fashioned from fire, that they move unseen, none of this is legend or guesswork. It is revelation, and every harder question in this room stands upon it.

Revelation names their element and their precedence, but it gives no dates and no dynasties for their age, and the Codex will not supply what it withheld.

2Corruption and BloodScholars differ

Here the register of evidence changes, and the Codex says so plainly. What follows is not a hadith of the Prophet ﷺ; it is the memory of the early community, athar related from Ibn Abbas and other early narrators, recorded by at-Tabari in his commentary on 2:30 and gathered by Ibn Kathir in al-Bidaya wan-Nihaya and in his own tafsir. They relate that the jinn dwelt upon the earth before Adam, alayhis-salam, and that their long tenancy soured: they worked corruption in the land and shed one another’s blood, until the earth they had been granted was filled with both. The chains of these reports are graded variously by the scholars, some serviceable, some weak, and none of them carries the weight of prophetic speech. We tell the story because the early community preserved it; we mark its grade because they also taught us to.

Later tellings pad this age with durations, dynasties, and folklore categories such as al-Hinn and al-Binn; none of that is established, and it is left aside here.

3The Army Sent DownScholars differ

The same athar continue, and the same caution travels with them. When corruption had filled the land, the reports relate, Allah sent against the jinn an army of angels, and the age of fire on the open earth was broken. The angels drove them as wind drives smoke, out of the settled lands, until the survivors clung to the islands of the seas and the fringes of the mountains, the edges of the inhabited world. So the account stands in Tafsir at-Tabari on 2:30 and in Ibn Kathir’s al-Bidaya wan-Nihaya, from Ibn Abbas and others; and in some versions Iblis, then still a devoted worshipper, marched among that host or at its head. How large the army was, how long the fighting lasted, what names the fallen bore, the established reports do not say, and the Codex will not say it for them.

Every scene in this chapter rests on companion and tabi’i reports recorded by the exegetes, not on any marfu’ hadith of the Prophet ﷺ.

4The One Who RoseMixed evidence

One figure walks out of the debated chapters into the certain ones. The Qur’an settles his nature: ‘he was of the jinn, and he departed from the command of his Lord’ (18:50). Yet when the order to prostrate came, it was addressed to the angels, and the exception is counted from inside their ranks (2:34); the classical scholars hold the two truths in one breath, of the angels by company and worship, of the jinn by origin and fire. The athar add a quieter, unproven detail: that Iblis had risen against the corruptors of the first war, and that victory taught him to admire himself. That seed is debated. The harvest is not. When the command came, he measured himself against a creature of clay and said, ‘I am better than him; You created me from fire, and You created him from clay’ (7:12, 38:76). Whatever planted the pride, the Qur’an is witness that pride is what unmade him.

That the first war fed his pride is an athar-level detail of varying strength; that pride destroyed him is Qur’anic text.

5The Question of the AngelsMixed evidence

Now the Qur’an and the athar meet in a single verse. When your Lord said to the angels, ‘I am placing upon the earth a khalifah,’ they said, ‘Will You place upon it one who will work corruption in it and shed blood, while we glorify You with Your praise and proclaim Your holiness?’ He said, ‘Surely I know what you do not know’ (2:30). The question is certain; how the angels came to ask it is not, and the commentators are honest about the gap. At-Tabari and Ibn Kathir set out three readings side by side. Perhaps the angels had watched the jinn corrupt and bleed, and feared the sequel; so say the athar of the earlier chapters. Perhaps Allah had informed them of what this new creature would do. Perhaps they deduced it from the nature of earthly creatures themselves. No single answer is binding; the verse is revelation, and its backstory is interpretation.

At-Tabari and Ibn Kathir record all three explanations without declaring any one of them binding, and the Codex keeps their restraint.

6What RemainsMixed evidence

The first war did not end the jinn; it unseated them. The Qur’an lets them speak for themselves, long after: ‘among us are the righteous, and among us are other than that; we were on divergent paths’ (72:11); ‘among us are those who submit, and among us are the unjust’ (72:14). They believe and rebel beside us still, unseen tenants of the earth they once held alone. And the sky some of them once approached is sealed: they found it ‘filled with mighty guards and burning flames’, and whoever steals a hearing now finds ‘a flame lying in wait’ (72:8-9). The earth itself passed to a creature of clay who can corrupt it exactly as the angels feared, and can also carry the trust that the heavens declined (33:72). Read with all its grades showing, the first war leaves one lesson: the earth is inherited by worship, not by strength, and pride after victory is how the highest fall.

The Qur’an ties this sealing of the sky to the era of revelation, a sign that the story of the jinn runs on into our own.

The People of ’Adقَوْمُ عَادٍ

A people amplified in strength asked who could be mightier, and the wind answered for seven nights and eight days.

After the people of Nuh had passed, the Qur’an says, Allah made ’Ad successors in the earth and increased them abundantly in stature and strength. To them He sent their brother Hud, who asked nothing but that they worship Allah alone and remember the favor that had raised them. Their answer was the boast the Qur’an preserves, who is mightier than us in strength, and its reply is immediate: the One who created them is mightier. They clung to what their fathers served, and the argument closed with a promise of punishment they had dared their prophet to hasten.

The end came by wind, a barren, screaming wind that Allah imposed on them seven nights and eight days without pause. Surah al-Haqqah lets us see the morning after: a people thrown down as if they were hollow trunks of palm trees, and asks whether any remnant of them can be found. Surah al-Fajr names their glory, Iram of the pillars, the like of which had not been created in the lands, and al-Ahqaf names their home among the wind curved dunes. Strength, the passage teaches, is an argument for the Maker, never against Him.

How tall were they? The Qur’an says only that their creation was amplified, and it lets that suffice. Later reports circulated exact figures in cubits, sometimes vast ones, yet these travel through chains and lore whose soundness is debated, not through the Book or the established Sunnah, and this room does not state them as fact. What is established is the lesson ’Ad were built to carry: no measure of body or building has ever held back the command of Allah.

Thamud and the She-Camelثَمُودُ

To a people who carved mountains into homes, Allah sent a she-camel as a test, and one cry ended the trial.

When they demanded proof, the proof came walking: the she-camel of Allah, a clear sign from their Lord. The command was gentle and exact, let her eat in Allah’s earth, the water is shared between her and you, and do not touch her with harm. Surah ash-Shams tells the ending in five verses: the most wretched of them rose, the she-camel was hamstrung, and their Lord crushed them for their sin and made them level.

Surah al-Hijr calls them the companions of al-Hijr, who denied the messengers and carved secure homes from the mountains, till the cry seized them at morning and nothing they had earned availed them. Surah Hud adds the mercy inside the warning: after the she-camel was hamstrung they were granted three days in their homes, a promise not to be belied. Security cut from rock, the passage teaches, is still only rock; the only safety that morning belonged to Salih and those who believed with him.

The stone remembers builders; the Qur’an remembers why building was not enough.

The People of Nuh and the Long Patienceقَوْمُ نُوحٍ

Nine hundred and fifty years of calling ended with a door that closed as the sky’s gates opened.

The Qur’an gives the span itself: Nuh remained among his people a thousand years less fifty, calling them to their Lord. Surah Nuh preserves the shape of that patience, I called them night and day, openly and in secret; they pressed their fingers into their ears and pulled their garments over themselves and grew arrogant. He promised them what repentance buys, forgiveness, rain in abundance, wealth and sons, gardens and rivers. Generations were born, heard the same truthful voice, and taught their children to refuse it, clinging to Wadd and Suwa and the idols the Qur’an names.

Surah al-Qamar carries the ending in a rush of verses: they called Our servant a liar and said, madman, and he was rebuked, so he called his Lord, I am overcome, so give victory. Then the gates of the sky were opened with pouring water and the earth burst with springs, and the waters met by a decree already measured. Nuh was carried on a thing of planks and nails, running under Our eyes, a recompense for the one denied. Of his people the Qur’an says they were drowned for their sins and made to enter a Fire; it was left as a sign, the surah says, so is there any who will remember?

How far did the water reach? The Qur’an fixes the moral scope with perfect clarity, the people who denied Nuh were destroyed, none of the believers were lost, and Nuh prayed that no dweller from the disbelievers be left upon the earth. What it does not give is a map. Whether the flood covered the whole planet or the whole of Nuh’s people and their lands is a question scholars have discussed, and this entry marks it debated rather than deciding it. The certainty is the lesson: denial drowned, and gratitude sailed.

The Ghoul: a Belief Correctedلَا غُولَ

Pre-Islamic Arabia populated the night desert with a monster, and the Prophet ﷺ dismissed it in two words.

The Arabs before Islam told of the ghul, a desert creature that shifted shapes, lit false fires and led travellers off the road to devour them. Whole journeys were narrated around escaping it. The correction is therefore precise, not sweeping. It removes a superstition. It does not touch the jinn, who are established by the Qur’an with separate evidence, and it leaves travellers with dhikr rather than dread.

The narrators do not explain how faith can fill a vessel, and the Prophet ﷺ did not elaborate; the report is left in its strangeness, as revelation sometimes is. What the night would ask of him was not courage alone but certainty, the settled knowledge that what the eye was about to see was true and from his Lord. Wisdom and faith were poured into him the way provision is given to a traveler, measured for the road ahead: seven heavens, the prophets in their stations, the Lote Tree at the limit of things, and words spoken beyond it.

4The Arrival of al-BuraqSunnah-confirmed

He was brought a mount whom the narrations call al-Buraq: a white beast, smaller than a mule and larger than a donkey, placing its hoof at the farthest point its sight could reach. The authentic reports say nothing more of its form, and restraint honors them; whatever later storytelling added belongs to imagination, not transmission. Distance, for such a creature, was a matter of glances. The Prophet ﷺ mounted, with Jibril beside him, and the road to Jerusalem shrank beneath that stride. In the narration of Anas he tied al-Buraq at the ring by the gate of the sanctuary, the same ring, he said, where the prophets before him had tied it, one small gesture binding this journey to every journey heaven had ever commanded.

5From the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest MosqueQur’an-confirmed

The Qur’an itself carries this stage of the journey, in words recited in every generation since: Glorified is He who took His servant by night from the Sacred Mosque to the Farthest Mosque, whose surroundings We have blessed, that We might show him of Our signs. Each word is weighed. It happened by night, in secrecy and stillness; he traveled as His servant, the title of deepest nearness; and the road ran first to al-Aqsa, sanctuary of the earlier prophets, before it turned upward. Makkah and Jerusalem were joined in a single night, and this ummah has understood ever since that its story does not begin with itself. The Prophet ﷺ entered a line of prophecy older than empires, and prayed where his brothers had prayed.

The majority of scholars hold the journey took place with body and soul together, awake, which is why Quraysh found the claim so enormous; a minority of early voices spoke of vision, and the commentaries record the discussion.

6The Prayer of the ProphetsSunnah-confirmed

At Bayt al-Maqdis he entered the sanctuary and prayed two rak’ahs. In the narration of Abu Hurayrah, the time of prayer came and he led them, the assembled company of prophets gathered in rows behind one imam from the descendants of Isma’il alayhis-salam. The reports linger on no ceremony; the scene is given in a line, as if its meaning needed no help. Every messenger had carried the one call through his own people and his own age; on this night the callers themselves stood shoulder to shoulder, and the Prophet ﷺ of the final nation stood in front. Leadership in prayer is the quietest of honors, and it was granted quietly, in the sanctuary that had held prophecy for centuries.

Scholars differ over whether this congregational prayer occurred before the ascent or after the return; the transmitted wordings admit both arrangements.

7The Choice Between Milk and WineSunnah-confirmed

Two vessels were set before him, one of milk and one of wine. He took the milk, and Jibril said: praise be to Allah who guided you to the fitrah; had you taken the wine, your ummah would have gone astray. The test was placed at the hinge of the journey, before the heavens opened. Milk is the first food of every human life, nourishment without confusion, sweetness that clarifies; wine is sweetness that unseats the mind. The choice was made once, on behalf of everyone who would afterward belong to him. In the Prophet’s ﷺ steady hand reaching for the plainer cup, the temper of the final religion was quietly fixed: a way aligned with how human beings are made.

Bukhari 3887 places offered vessels, wine, milk, and in some wordings honey, at Sidrat al-Muntaha, while Bukhari 5576 sets two vessels at Bayt al-Maqdis; scholars allow that both offerings occurred.

8The First Gate of HeavenSunnah-confirmed

Then the ascent, the Mi’raj, began. At the lowest heaven Jibril asked for the gate to be opened, and a voice asked: who is this? Jibril. And who is with you? Muhammad ﷺ. Has he been sent for? He has. Then the gate was opened, and the keeper said: welcome to him, and what an excellent coming is his. The exchange repeats at every heaven, and the narrations preserve it each time without shortening it, seven questions and seven welcomes. Heaven does not open by right of arrival but by invitation; even the best of creation entered as one sent for. Above the first gate the unseen world stood arranged in its ranks, waiting to receive its guest.

9Adam and the Two Directions of HumanitySunnah-confirmed

In the first heaven sat Adam alayhis-salam, the father of humankind. He greeted the Prophet ﷺ as a righteous son and a righteous prophet. In the narration of Abu Dharr, multitudes of souls were arrayed on his right and on his left; when he looked to his right he smiled, and when he looked to his left he wept. These were the souls of his descendants: the people of Paradise on the one hand, the people of the Fire on the other. The first ancestor still watches the long procession of his children, rejoicing and grieving by turns. No scene in the ascent is more human than this. The journey through the heavens begins with a father’s face, moved by what becomes of his family.

10Yahya and IsaSunnah-confirmed

In the second heaven he met the two maternal cousins, Yahya alayhis-salam and Isa alayhis-salam, son of Zakariyya alayhis-salam and son of Maryam. Each welcomed him as a righteous brother and a righteous prophet. The narrations pause on nothing further, yet the pairing carries weight of its own: two prophets of the house of Imran, one slain unjustly, one denied and then raised beyond reach, now standing together in peace above the world that refused them. For the Prophet ﷺ, walking out of his own years of rejection, the second heaven held a quiet reassurance. Those wronged for the sake of the message do not end inside the wrong done to them; they end in welcome, in company, in light.

11Yusuf and BeautySunnah-confirmed

In the third heaven stood Yusuf alayhis-salam. In the narration of Anas the Prophet ﷺ said of him: he had been given half of all beauty. The line is spoken plainly, without envy and without explanation. Yusuf’s beauty had once been his trial, the cause of the pit, the caravan, and the prison; here it stands transfigured, a mark of honor in a station of honor. The tradition understands his portion as half of beauty itself, and understands too that the fairest of faces was matched by the purest patience. He welcomed the Prophet ﷺ as a brother and a prophet and prayed for his good. What the world once sold at a low price, heaven displays at its true worth.

12Idris and the High StationSunnah-confirmed

In the fourth heaven he met Idris alayhis-salam, and the narration itself reaches for the Qur’an to describe him: We raised him to a high station. Little is transmitted of Idris beyond truthfulness and prophethood, and the ascent adds no legend to him; it simply shows the verse fulfilled, a man raised, in the plainest sense, into the heights. There is a lesson in the economy of it. Some of Allah’s servants are known through volumes; some are summarized in a single verse and a single greeting. Idris welcomed the Prophet ﷺ as the others had, righteous brother, righteous prophet, and the travelers passed on upward, through a station whose name the Qur’an had given before the night confirmed it.

Some transmissions vary which heaven a given prophet occupies; this sequence follows the narration of Malik ibn Sa’sa’ah shared by Bukhari and Muslim.

13HarunSunnah-confirmed

In the fifth heaven was Harun alayhis-salam, brother of Musa alayhis-salam and the voice that had steadied a nation while its deliverer stood on the mountain. He greeted the Prophet ﷺ with the words of welcome the others had used, a righteous brother and a righteous prophet. The narrations grant him no speech beyond the greeting, and the silence suits him; his whole prophethood had been a ministry of support, eloquence placed in the service of another man’s mission. In the ordering of the heavens he stands one level beneath his brother, near him beyond the world as he was within it. For a Prophet ﷺ who would soon lean hard on Musa’s counsel, meeting the helper just before the counselor carries its own gentle fitness.

14MusaSunnah-confirmed

In the sixth heaven he met Musa, and here the narration slows. When the Prophet ﷺ moved past him toward the seventh heaven, Musa wept. Asked why, he answered: I weep because a young man was sent after me whose ummah will enter Paradise in greater numbers than mine. The scholars read those tears with care: not envy, which has no life in that world, but the sorrow of a shepherd who wanted more for his own flock. Musa had carried Banu Isra’il through sea, desert, and forty years of wandering, and he loved them still. His weeping honors both nations at once. No one in the heavens will engage the Prophet ﷺ more than Musa, and the tears are only the first sign of his care.

15Ibrahim and the Frequented HouseSunnah-confirmed

In the seventh heaven, nearest the boundary of created things, he found Ibrahim alayhis-salam, the intimate friend of Allah, reclining against al-Bayt al-Ma’mur, the Frequented House. Ibrahim greeted him as a righteous son and a righteous prophet, the words Adam had spoken at the first heaven, so that the ascent is framed by two fathers: the father of humankind at its floor, the father of prophets at its summit. The House he leaned against stands above the heavens as the Ka’bah stands upon the earth, one axis of worship running through all the worlds. The man who had raised the foundations of the earthly House now rests beside its heavenly likeness, and between the two Houses the whole religion is strung like a single thread.

16The Seventy Thousand Who Never ReturnSunnah-confirmed

Of al-Bayt al-Ma’mur he was told: every day seventy thousand angels enter it, and those who leave never return to it; it is the last they will ever see of it. The number is not ornament, it is a window onto the scale of the unseen. Creation is vaster than any census, and its worship never repeats. Every day a new host, every day a farewell, from before Adam until the ending of days, and no angel comes twice. On earth, worshippers circle the Ka’bah and come back year after year; the heavens are so thick with servants that a single visit is a lifetime’s share. The report leaves the mind at the edge of a number it cannot see over, which is where minds belong before the unseen.

Bukhari 3887Muslim 162
17Sidrat al-MuntahaMixed evidence

Then he was raised to Sidrat al-Muntaha, the Lote Tree of the farthest limit, which the Qur’an names as the station of a second encounter with revelation. The narration reaches for the largest familiar things to describe it: fruits like the great jars of Hajar, leaves like the ears of elephants. From its base issued four rivers, two hidden and two apparent; the hidden pair, he was told, are rivers of Paradise, and the apparent pair are named as the Nile and the Euphrates. Here the knowledge of every created being finds its ceiling; what rises from below ends at this tree, and what descends from above is received at it. Then it was covered by that which covered it, and it changed, until none of Allah’s creation could have described its beauty.

Commentators, an-Nawawi among them, read the naming of the Nile and the Euphrates as a statement of the blessing attached to their lands rather than a map of their springs.

18What No Created Tongue Can DescribeScholars differ

What happened at the summit is guarded by the Qur’an’s own reticence: the heart did not lie about what it saw; the sight neither swerved nor overreached. Here the first generations themselves differed, and honesty requires saying so. Aisha, the Mother of the Believers, held that whoever claims the Prophet ﷺ saw his Lord has erred gravely; what he saw in his true form was Jibril, and the verses of an-Najm describe that vision. Ibn Abbas held that he saw Him, and in one wording, saw Him with his heart, twice. Later scholars often joined the two: a vision of the heart affirmed, a vision of the eyes denied, and the essence of Allah unseen by anyone in this life. The summit of the ascent stays, fittingly, beyond created description.

Both positions are preserved in Sahih Muslim; the disagreement concerns the vision at the summit, never the reality of the ascent itself.

19Paradise and KawtharSunnah-confirmed

He was shown Paradise. The narration of Malik ibn Sa’sa’ah gives two details and stops: domes of pearl, and soil of musk. In another authentic report the Prophet ﷺ said: while I was walking in Paradise I came upon a river whose banks were vaults of hollowed pearl, and I asked Jibril, what is this? He said: this is al-Kawthar, which your Lord has given you. The shortest surah of the Qur’an had promised that abundance in a single breath; the night showed it flowing. The descriptions end early, and rightly. Of the home of the righteous we are handed the scent of its earth, the curve of one riverbank, and the certainty that it is real, prepared, and waiting.

20Warnings Shown in the UnseenMixed evidence

The journey also carried warnings. The strongest of these reports concerns the backbiters: the Prophet ﷺ passed a people with nails of copper, tearing at their own faces and chests, and was told: these are the ones who ate the flesh of people and assaulted their honor. Abu Dawud transmits it with a sound chain. Other punishment scenes in circulation, the devourers of usury, the neglecters of prayer, and more, rest on narrations of varying strength, and several belong to a separate, authentic dream of the Prophet ﷺ rather than to this night; popular retellings have braided the two together. Precision here is itself reverence. What stands firm is the emphasis: on the night of nearness, sins of the tongue against absent people were singled out for horror.

The backbiters scene is Abu Dawud 4878 and is graded strong; several widely retold scenes come instead from the separate dream narration in Bukhari 7047 or from weak chains, and each must be weighed on its own.

21Fifty PrayersSunnah-confirmed

Above the Lote Tree the obligation was given: fifty prayers in every day and night. The Prophet ﷺ accepted and turned homeward. There was no bargaining at the summit; the servant received what his Lord ordained, and the number itself declared how much of a human life is owed to remembrance. Fifty prayers would have threaded worship through every waking hour, a life with no seam between devotion and the rest. That, in truth, is the scale of the debt. What follows in the story is not a lightening of that truth but a mercy in its accounting, and like every mercy of this night it arrives through love: the love of an old prophet for a nation he had never met.

22The Counsel of MusaSunnah-confirmed

Descending, he passed Musa, who asked: what were you commanded? Fifty prayers each day, he said. Musa answered: your ummah will not manage it; I tried people before you, and I treated Banu Isra’il with the hardest treatment; return to your Lord and ask Him to lighten it for your ummah. So the Prophet ﷺ returned, and a portion was removed. Musa sent him back, and back again, the narration patient with every repetition, until five remained. The scene honors both men: the elder prophet spending his hard experience on a nation not his own, and the final Prophet ﷺ unashamed to stand before his Lord again and again as a pleader for his people. Between Musa’s realism and Muhammad’s ﷺ asking, the shape of the ummah’s day was settled.

The narrations differ on whether the reductions came ten at a time or five at a time; the endpoint of five is agreed upon in every sound wording.

23Five Prayers with the Reward of FiftySunnah-confirmed

When five remained, Musa urged him to ask once more. The Prophet ﷺ answered: I have asked my Lord until I am shy before Him; I am content, and I submit. Then a caller announced: I have carried through My obligation and lightened the burden on My servants; they are five, and they are fifty; the word is not changed with Me. Five in performance, fifty in reward, each prayer weighed at ten, the arithmetic of a generosity that had intended mercy from the first. The final number was not wrung from heaven; it was a gift heaven had prepared, given in a way that let one prophet counsel, another plead, and a people know forever how they were loved. He came down carrying the religion’s daily heartbeat.

24The Return Before DawnMixed evidence

He was returned to Makkah before dawn had broken over it, the whole immensity folded into a fraction of one night. The authentic narrations spend no words on the homeward road; a journey that outran description outran its narrators too. Morning found the Prophet ﷺ among his people carrying the heaviest of tidings: that he had been, in a single night, to Jerusalem and beyond the seventh heaven. The sira adds that he spoke of caravans passed on the way and the hour of their arrival, details his hearers could test; those reports vary in strength and are read as supporting detail, not foundation. The foundation was starker: one truthful man’s word against the whole weight of the possible.

25The Test of Al-AqsaSunnah-confirmed

By morning the claim was in the open, and Quraysh received it as a gift, the perfect absurdity with which to finish him. Men who had ridden a month to reach Syria knew what one night meant. Those who had seen Jerusalem pressed him to describe it. The Prophet ﷺ later told what happened: when Quraysh called me a liar, I stood in al-Hijr, and Allah displayed Bayt al-Maqdis before me, and I began to tell them of its signs while I was looking at it. He had crossed it by night, his gaze set on higher things; the examiners wanted its doors and windows. So the sanctuary was raised before his eyes, and he answered from the original. The test built to break the story became part of its proof.

26Abu Bakr As-SiddiqMixed evidence

The report reached Abu Bakr as a taunt: your companion claims he went by night to Bayt al-Maqdis and returned before morning. His answer, preserved in the report of al-Hakim and others, wasted no breath on geography: if he said it, then he has spoken the truth. And he went further: I believe him in what is more distant than that; I believe him about news from heaven arriving in a morning or an evening. From that day, the tradition holds, he was called as-Siddiq, the ever-affirming. The grading of the naming report is discussed by the scholars, and its meaning is beyond dispute in the memory of the ummah. Faith of this kind does not verify claims one by one; it has already verified the man.

The naming report is in al-Hakim’s Mustadrak, graded sahih by him with adh-Dhahabi concurring; it is not in Bukhari or Muslim, though the disbelief of Quraysh that occasioned it is.

27The Gift Carried by Every MuslimSunnah-confirmed

Every other ordinance came down to the earth; the prayer alone was given at the summit, received without any intermediary space between the Giver and the one receiving. That is its measure. In the narration of Ibn Mas’ud, three things were given that night: the five prayers, the closing verses of Surat al-Baqarah, and the promise of forgiveness for whoever meets Allah without associating anything with Him. Of the whole journey, the heavens, the Tree, the rivers, the welcomes, this is what the Prophet ﷺ carried home. Fourteen centuries on, a tired worker rising before first light to pray fajr is holding the night’s gift. Five times a day, in every land on earth, some part of this night returns: a believer turns toward the qiblah, and the road opened in the Year of Sorrow opens again.
