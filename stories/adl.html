Al-Adl · The Perfectly Just

العَدْل

The Second Record

Abbasid Baghdad, the 800s

Before you begin

The wrong in this story is never put right in this world; read it when yours has not been either.

I wrote what happened. So did One who does not smudge.

A Pen in the House of Judgment

Baghdad in those years was the heaviest city in the world, and the busiest, and the loudest at noon: the round city of the caliphs aging at its center, the markets of Karkh roaring on the west bank, the Tigris between them carrying rafts of skins loaded with dates, tar, pottery, soldiers, melons, grief, everything. Paper had come to the city within living memory, and the city had fallen on it like a hungry man on bread. There were mills for it now, and a street of sellers, and in the offices of the state the scratching of pens had become the sound of power itself.

Thabit was a porter’s son from the Karkh side who had been caught, at nine years old, writing the names of dates in the dust of his father’s stall in a hand so clean that a passing teacher stopped and stared. The kuttab, the boys’ school, took him for the sake of that hand. The offices took him at seventeen. By twenty he was a junior katib, a scribe, in the court of a qadi, a judge, and he loved the work the way some men love the sea, without being able to say why the love went so deep.

It was his teacher, an old scribe with one clouded eye, who had set the nail in him years before. Paper forgets nothing, the old man said. So write nothing on it that wants forgetting, and never once make it lie. A lying page outlives its liar, and stands up on the Day the scrolls are laid open, and reads itself aloud. Thabit was thirteen when he heard that. Some sentences arrive before the life they are meant for, and wait.

His work was the mahdar, the written minute of what was said in court: claim, answer, testimony, oath. Above it stood the sijill, the judgment enrolled and sealed. He wrote quickly and exactly, and he believed with his whole young heart that the room he served was a scale, and the qadi’s pen its needle, and that between the ink and the truth there ran a marriage no power in Baghdad could divorce.

The qadi he served was old, learned, and tired, with thirty years of honest judgments in small matters behind him. He was honest the way most men are honest: in the amounts that cost little. This is not a rare kind of man. It is the commonest kind there is, and no one knows which kind he himself is until the day the amount is large.

Around the court moved the other Baghdad, the one that does not appear in the minutes: chamberlains of great houses, cousins of cousins, notes folded small and sealed with important rings, witnesses of professional reputation. The court kept a roll of men certified as udul, upright, whose word the law would accept. Udul is the plural of adl, justice; the word and the Name are kin. The court called those men just. Remember the roll of the upright, and remember what the word means, for this story turns on the distance between a word and its meaning.

Sixty Palms by the Water

Dawud the date seller had a stall in Karkh no wider than a prayer mat and a voice that could reach across the whole date market: amber ones, honey ones, black ones from my own palms, sweetness with a Bismillah in it. The sweetness was not a boast. He owned sixty palms on a canal below the city, planted by his father, and at day’s end he gave the bruised fruit to the porters’ children free, and everyone in that market had at some time been small, and remembered him.

The orchard had water on two sides, and shade, and a view down the running canal, and it was therefore beautiful, and it was therefore seen. The man who saw it had a cousin who kept the door of a palace, a stronger form of wealth in Baghdad than land. He offered Dawud a price that was an insult wearing the clothes of an offer. Dawud said: my father put sixty shoots into this ground with Bismillah over every one of them; find me the price of sixty Bismillahs and I will consider it. He said it mildly, and the market repeated it for a month, laughing warmly. It was the most expensive sentence of his life.

The claim, when it came, was beautiful in its workmanship. A deed of sale, twenty years old by its date: the orchard sold to the claimant’s late father by Dawud’s late father, price received in full, possession deferred by family arrangement. Two witnesses from the roll of the udul, men with certified beards and certified reputations, stood forth and swore they had seen the signing with their own eyes, twenty years past. Dawud stood in the court with his mouth open, holding a water skin he had forgotten he was holding, and his one witness was an old neighbor who stammered under the eyes of the room and got a date wrong, and was dismissed.

It fell to Thabit to copy the deed into the mahdar, and this is where the case entered him like a blade, because paper was his craft and ink was his mother tongue. The deed claimed twenty years. But the sheet was of the smooth new stock the mill by the Syrian Gate had been making for less than a decade; he had a ream of its brothers on his own shelf.

And the ink. Gall ink is a living thing; it goes down black and then browns with the years toward rust, like iron, like blood. This ink was black as the state’s own banners, black as a crow in the rain. The folds were sharp. The worm had not found it. The deed was a young man wearing his grandfather’s turban, and to Thabit’s trained eyes it might as well have been laughing.

He went to the qadi at day’s end, privately, as a junior should, and laid the sheet in the lamplight and said what he saw, item by item, the paper, the ink, the folds, in the flat voice of his trade.

The qadi looked at the deed for a long time, and at the folded note with an important seal that lay on his own table, and then at nothing. The witnesses are certified, he said at last. Certification is the court’s eyes; I cannot see past my own instruments. Copy the record, Thabit. And then, softer, being for one moment merely an old man: they would not stop at taking my post. I have daughters. You will understand when the world has given you more to lose.

Thabit walked home along the canal through the smell of evening bread, and told his wife, because he told her everything. She was quiet while he spoke. Their daughter slept in the corner with a fist against her cheek. We ate barley before you served in that court, his wife said finally, which was her whole argument and both sides of it. Then she said the sentence he carried to his grave: do what you can pray behind.

What the Pen Refused

Judgment was given on a Thursday, in a full room, the claimant’s people in linen worth more than the orchard’s year, Dawud in washed cotton with his hands empty. The witnesses swore again, smooth as pulleys. The qadi ruled the deed proven and the orchard passed, and the room made the small sound rooms make when a thing everyone predicted has finished happening: not a gasp, only a settling, like poured grain.

Then the clerk of the court was to enroll the judgment, and the clerk of the court did not move. Thabit sat with the reed in his hand and the sijill open before him, and the room waited, and he heard his own voice arrive from a distance, level, as if dictating to itself: I cannot write that this court found the truth. I copied the deed. Its paper is younger than my daughter, and its ink is younger than her teeth. Let the record show the witnesses certified and the paper new, and let whoever reads it weigh both.

For a moment the room was perfectly still, and in that stillness something very old and very large seemed to lean over it and look in.

What happened then happened quickly, the way such things do. He was removed from the room by two bailiffs, not roughly, the way you remove a lamp that is smoking. Another hand enrolled the sijill before the ink was dry. Within the week his name was struck from the diwan lists, every office of the state closed to him at a single stroke, and a soft voiced man he had never met stood beside him in the mosque courtyard and observed that a scribe who will not write is a knife that will not cut, and that both go into the drawer, and that he should thank Allah it was only the drawer. Thabit thanked Allah, though not for that.

On the steps of the court, before all of it descended, Dawud had caught his sleeve. The date seller’s face was grey, but his eyes were steady, and what he said was this: you lost your bread for my palms, and neither of us saved them. Allah feed you from a table I cannot see. And when Thabit began some apology for the uselessness of it, Dawud shook his head. The Prophet ﷺ said that between the du’a of the wronged and Allah there is no veil, he said. Be easy. I am not praying against anyone. A man with one arrow does not spend it in the market. I am saving mine for the other Court.

The judgment stood. Understand this, because the story will not soften it later: it stood. It stands, as far as the records of this world run, to this day.

The Years of Barley Bread

The orchard’s fate was quick. Within two years the new owner had felled forty of the sixty palms, because palms drop dates and dates draw wasps and wasps trouble guests, and had raised a pavilion of cedar with rose plots to the water’s edge, and hung lamps in the trees that were left, whose fruit no one picked. Pleasure boats tied there at night, and the music carried across the canal to the road where the fruit carts passed.

Dawud carried loads in the date market until his back bent, and died in a fever year, and was buried in the common ground, with a date palm frond laid on the bier by the porters’ children, who had grown, and remembered.

For Thabit there began the years of barley bread. No diwan would touch him; the stroke that struck his name had been thorough. He set a reed mat by the gate of a Friday mosque and became a letter writer, a young man among the old men of that trade, taking dictation from mothers whose sons had gone for soldiers in Khurasan, drawing up bills of sale for onion men, marriage lists for brides whose fathers counted the dowry twice. He was paid in copper fals, sometimes in eggs, once a winter cloak. He wrote each letter as if it were a sijill of the empire, because exactness was the one wealth no one could strike from any list.

Poverty is not dramatic; that is its power. It is barley instead of wheat, one lamp lit only for the Qur’an and mending done twice, a daughter married with a dowry raised by selling the good brass pen case, a wife whose hands took in spinning and whose mouth never once, in forty years, said the words you could have stayed silent. Once he asked her outright if she regretted the day the pen refused. She was quiet the length of three breaths. I regret the pavilion, she said. I do not regret you. Eat your bread.

For six years he went to the mazalim, the court of complaints where the wronged of the empire could in principle hand their grievance upward past every corrupted instrument. He stood in lines among men who had walked from other provinces holding petitions like sick children. Twice his paper was taken, stamped, and drowned somewhere upstream. Once a young clerk he had himself trained avoided his eyes with such labor that Thabit pitied him and left the line. Petitions rose in that office the way water rises: they did not. In the seventh year he stopped going, not because his certainty had died, but because he finally understood the address was wrong.

The man whose cousin kept the palace door lived long, gave banquets, and built at a crossroads of Karkh a public fountain, from which the quarter drank and called him generous. Thabit passed that fountain on foot for thirty years. Once, in a hot Ramadan twilight, to teach his heart something he could not name, he made himself drink from it, and stood with the sweet water on his lips while the man’s carved name looked down at him from the stone. He was never sure what the lesson had been, only that his heart came away from it quieter, the way a fever breaks in the night without anyone seeing the hour.

But do not let the quietness deceive you; there was a war in him, and it lasted years. There were nights he laid his forehead on the mat and could not find one word to send up, nights he asked outright: where was Your court, ya Rabb, my Lord, when the only door above that door was Yours? You saw the ink. You made the eyes I saw it with.

No answer came. What came, in its own time, was the ordinary dawn: the adhan over the roofs, bread, his wife’s hand on his shoulder, the pen to be cut, the mat to be swept. He understood nothing more than before. He was held no less than before. He learned, slowly, the difference between an answer and a floor, and that the second is what a man actually stands on, all his life.

The Record That Does Not Smudge

One thing he had done, the night of the judgment, and told no one but his wife. He had sat till the lamp burned out and written, in his exact court hand, a full and true record of the case: the claim, the deed, the paper of the deed, the ink of the deed, the folds, the names of the witnesses from the roll of the upright, what was said in the lamplight and by whom, and the judgment as it was enrolled by another hand.

He sealed it in a reed tube against damp, and set it in a niche of the wall, and waited for the reviewing qadi, the honest successor, the day of the reopened file. Qadis came and went. The wrong outlived them all, as wrongs bred in high places do; they are hardy stock. The tube stayed in the wall forty years, and he called it, privately, his second record.

In his old age his hands took the shake, and his daughter’s son, Ayyub, a sharp boy with his grandfather’s exact eyes, learned the pen beside him at the mosque gate and grew furious with the story when he was old enough to be told it. The heirs of that house still hold the land, the boy said. Take out the tube. There are new offices now, new men, I will carry it myself.

Thabit looked at his grandson with the long look of a man counting what a thing will cost the person he loves, and said: the men who did it are all before Allah already, and the men who hold it now bought roses, not palms; they know nothing. And then, seeing the boy’s face: you think I am letting the wrong die. Sit down, and I will show you where it is kept.

He took the tube to the river at dawn, the boy beside him carrying the wudu jug, past the fountain with the dead man’s name on it, down to the brown Tigris where the rafts went by loaded with dates from someone’s palms somewhere. He broke the seal and read it over once, the whole of it, in a steady voice, to the water and the boy and the gulls, the way the record of a case is read before a judge.

Then he said: I wrote what happened. So did One who does not smudge. Mine has waited fifty years for a judge with clean hands. His does not wait; it is already entered, and the session is already appointed, and the witnesses cannot be bought, for the witnesses are the skins of the accused themselves.

He let the river take the paper. It rode the current a little way, turning, pale on the brown water, and went under without ceremony, and nothing fell out of the world. That is what the boy said he remembered best, telling it years later as an old scribe himself: that he had braced himself to feel the truth drown, and instead felt the morning become very wide and very light, as if a debt had been transferred to a House that could carry it, and the porters released.

They prayed Fajr on the bank, the old man and the boy, on one spread cloak. In the prostration Thabit stayed down a long time, and Ayyub, glancing sideways as boys do, saw the old lips moving and thought he was pleading, and only understood decades later that he had been doing the other thing: he had been handing something over, item by item, palms and pavilion and fountain and pen, into the custody of the only Court that had never once lost his respect.

He died poor, in the month of Shaban, owing no one, owed by this world more than it ever paid. The mosque of the gate where he had written letters for forty years could not hold the janazah crowd, which would have astonished him: mothers of soldiers, onion men, porters, brides grown grey, a whole city of small correct documents come walking behind their scribe.

There was no vindication in it. The orchard is a garden of someone else’s roses to this day, and no record in any office of this world says otherwise. The scales of Baghdad went on weighing short, as the scales of every Baghdad do. And above the whole patient city, unhurried, unbought, exact to the weight of a mustard seed, the other Court kept its records, and kept its appointment. It keeps them still.

Ask why the scribe was never repaid, and you have asked the question the story cannot answer and the Name can. Al-Adl does not mean the One who makes every court honest by Thursday. It means the One before whom every Thursday is still open, every file recoverable, every mustard seed weighed: the Judge who was never for one instant deceived, and never for one instant late, whose session no cousin can enter through the side door, because the Judge has no cousins, and His house has no side doors.

وَنَضَعُ الْمَوَازِينَ الْقِسْطَ لِيَوْمِ الْقِيَامَةِ فَلَا تُظْلَمُ نَفْسٌ شَيْئًا ۖ وَإِن كَانَ مِثْقَالَ حَبَّةٍ مِّنْ خَرْدَلٍ أَتَيْنَا بِهَا ۗ وَكَفَىٰ بِنَا حَاسِبِينَ

And We will set the scales of justice for the Day of Resurrection, and no soul will be wronged in anything. And if there be the weight of a mustard seed, We will bring it forth. And sufficient are We as reckoners.
