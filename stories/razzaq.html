Ar-Razzaq · The Provider

الرَّزَّاق

The Baker of Small Loaves

Cairo, early 1200s, a famine year when the Nile failed to rise

Before you begin

For anyone who has looked into the bottom of the jar and wondered how it could possibly stretch to the end of the month.

He weighed the flour every night of his life; he never once managed to weigh the Giver.

The Year the River Forgot

In the year the river forgot to rise, the criers of Cairo came up from the Nilometer on Rawda island with the mark of the water, and for the first time in living memory their voices were low. Every summer of his life, Yunus the baker had heard those voices climb with the flood: fourteen cubits, fifteen, sixteen, and then drums, and the cutting of the canal, and boys leaping into brown water with their clothes on. This year the number stopped short, like a sentence nobody wanted to finish. The men in the flour market did their sums in silence. A river that does not rise is a field that will not drink, and a field that will not drink is bread that will not be.

Yunus ibn Dawud kept an oven in a lane off the saddlers’ market, near the southern gate. It was not a famous oven. It had baked no bread for princes and held no secrets except one: a lump of old dough his father had kept alive for forty years and Yunus for twenty more, the sour mother from which every batch was born. Each night he folded a fistful of the day’s dough back into the jar, and each dawn it had grown in the dark, quietly, the way faithful things grow. He had a wife, Safiyya, a son of nine called Dawud, a daughter of six called Ruqayya, and a trade that put him elbow deep in other people’s hunger.

The famine did not arrive like a storm. It leaked in, like water under a door. First the price of wheat doubled, and people joked about it. Then it doubled again, and they stopped. Barley appeared in loaves that had been proud of their whiteness. The villages upstream began to empty, and thin families drifted to the city gates with cooking pots and nothing to put in them. By winter, the saddlers’ lane had learned a new quiet: the quiet of streets where nobody is selling anything sweet, and the children have stopped shouting because shouting spends strength.

Cairo in a good year is a city you can smell before you can see: frying oil, cumin, dung smoke, river mud, sugar. That winter the smells went out one by one, like lamps. What remained was dust, and the cold ash odour of ovens that had gone dark. Of the fourteen bakers between the two markets, five had shut their doors by the month of Rajab. Their flour had run out, or their courage had. Yunus stayed lit. He told himself it was stubbornness. It was, at first.

Safiyya ran the house the way a pilot runs a boat through rocks, by exact knowledge and no sentiment. She knew the weight of a week in lentils, the price of a winter in oil, and precisely how many cupped handfuls of flour stood between her children and the door of the unthinkable. She had sold one of her dowry bracelets in autumn, quietly, and told no one, and the coins had gone into the flour bin one measure at a time. She was not hard. She was accurate, and in a famine, accuracy is a kind of love.

Yunus had been a boy in the last great dearth, thirty years before. He remembered almost none of it, except this: some mornings, in the worst of it, there had been barley bread on the doorstep. Round rough loaves, still warm, left before the dawn prayer by someone who never once let himself be seen. His father searched for the giver for years and never found him. He would say, to the end of his life, with wonder in his voice: we ate three winters on bread we never bought.

The market inspector, the muhtasib, still made his rounds that winter with his scales and his ledger, because law does not stop being law when it becomes hard. The weight of a loaf was fixed. What some bakers did to that weight was their own affair between themselves and the ovens of a later Day: chalk in the flour, dust in the barley, loaves with clean faces and dirty hearts. Yunus weighed true. He could not have told you why it mattered so much to him, except that bread was the one sermon he knew how to give.

Smaller Loaves

What he did instead was shrink the loaf. Not the weight the law fixed for a fals, that stayed honest to the grain. He shrank his own portioning, the margin every baker keeps for himself, the widow’s handful here, the cracked loaves there, the difference between what the oven made and what the house needed. He baked smaller for his own table so that the tray at the shop window could stay full longer into the afternoon, so that the last man in the line, the one with the look, would not have to turn around and tell his family a lie.

And then, because smallness has its own arithmetic, he began the night batch. After the last prayer, when the lane slept, he swept the day’s corners: bran, broken grain, the coarse siftings the pigeon keepers used to buy, the ends of barley the merchant let go for almost nothing because almost nothing was all Yunus offered. He kneaded it with the sour mother and a pinch of salt and baked it in the falling heat of the banked oven, rough loaves, dark and heavy and honest, bread with its sleeves rolled up.

Then he went out. There is an hour before the dawn prayer when Cairo belongs to the cats and the water carriers, and Yunus learned it by heart. He learned doors the way he knew dough, by feel: the schoolteacher who had not been paid since summer, the porter with six children and a back going crooked, the widow Umm Hasan whose cough came through her shutters like a slow drum. He set the loaves on the doorsteps and left. He was never once seen, or so he believed, and belief before dawn is easy.

Understand that he was not brave. His heart knocked at every corner. Some nights he stood in his own doorway with the basket on his arm and argued with himself in the dark: the jar is low, the winter is long, your children eat what you give away. He went anyway, not because the argument was won but because his feet had stopped listening to it. His father had loved one saying of the Prophet ﷺ above all others and had repeated it over the kneading trough for forty years: guard yourself from the Fire, even with half a date. Yunus had no dates. He had bran.

He paid for it where no one could see. He began eating his own supper short, then shorter. He mended his belt twice that winter, moving the buckle inward, and told Safiyya the leather had stretched. He drank warm water with a little salt at night, an old bakers’ trick against the hollow hour. Hunger, he discovered, is loud for three days and then becomes a companion, walking beside you with its hand on your shoulder, saying little.

The secrecy he kept for three reasons, though he could have named only one. For the takers, because shame is heavier than hunger, and a man who is seen receiving eats his bread with a bent neck. For himself, because he did not trust his own heart within a mile of praise. And for Safiyya, because she counted, and he could not face the count. This third reason he did not say even inwardly, but it set the hour of his errand as surely as the dawn set the hour of his prayer.

A loaf is a small thing. At the bottom of a hungry year it is not small. It is the difference between a child who sleeps and a child who cries himself there, and every mother on the lane could have told you which house had which.

✦

What Safiyya Counted

Safiyya kept her ledger without paper. Inside the lid of the flour bin she made small charcoal strokes, one for every measure that left it, and at her waist she wore a cord in which she tied a knot for every coin that left the house. She knew dough the way Yunus knew fire, and by the middle of winter the numbers had stopped agreeing. The bin was falling faster than the family ate. Two measures a night, near enough, walking out of the house on their own legs.

She did not accuse him at once. She was a careful woman, and careful women check the sum twice. She lay still one night with her breathing slow and false, and heard him rise, and heard the basket, and heard the door. When he came back in the black cold before the dawn prayer, she was standing by the oven with the charcoal lid in her hand, and the look on her face was not anger. Anger would have been easier. It was fear, counted out in small strokes.

“I have weighed the children this winter,” she said, quietly, so as not to wake them. “Not on scales. On my arms. Dawud is lighter than he was at harvest. Ruqayya’s wrists, I can close my finger and thumb around them and there is room. You are a good man, Yunus, and you are pouring water out of a boat that is leaking. When the jar is empty, will the lane feed us? The lane cannot feed itself. I am not asking you to be cruel. I am asking you to be arithmetic.”

He had no answer that could stand up in the court she kept. He was not a scholar; he could not have argued provision from the Book with chapter and verse. All he had was a memory with flour on it. “When I was Dawud’s age,” he said, “someone fed us three winters and was never seen. My father died still praying for a man whose face he did not know. Somebody’s wife counted those measures, Safiyya. Somebody’s wife watched that flour walk out the door in the dark.” He looked at the lid in her hand. “I never wondered until tonight what she said to him.”

“Perhaps she said nothing,” said Safiyya, “and they starved in the spring, and were praised at their funeral.” The words came out harder than her face wanted them to. In the silence after, the widow’s cough came through the wall, slow and patient as a drum. Safiyya closed her eyes. “The children first,” she said. “Promise me the children eat first, whatever else you do.” It was not surrender, and it was not permission. It was a woman moving one plank of her boat to see if the sea truly came in.

He stopped for three nights, all the same, to honour her fear, and those were the longest nights of the winter. The house felt no fuller for it. He lay awake doing sums of his own, and found himself counting backwards for once: the fever Ruqayya had shaken off in autumn when the physician had waived his fee, shrugging, saying bakers’ children were his alms. The fuel merchant who had thrown a sack of dung cakes onto the cart unasked. The sour mother itself, forty years unbought. He had kept accounts of what left the house all his life without noticing that he had never once been the only one providing for it.

On the fourth morning he saw the gleaners at his ash pit, two children with a spoon, going through the cold cinders for crusts that had burnt and been thrown away. They were not from the lane. They did not beg. They worked the ash with the seriousness of clerks, and it was this, the seriousness, that finished him. He came home and said to Safiyya, to her face and in daylight, “I will bake the night batch from my own bowl. Not yours, not theirs. Mine. I can be hungry. I cannot be the man who watched.”

She looked at him for a long time, and then she did the thing he loved her for until the day he died: she reached into the bin, and added one small measure to his. “Someone in this house must count,” she said. “Then count everything,” he said. “Count what comes in, too.” She thought this was a foolish answer, that winter. She tied a second cord at her waist anyway, because she was accurate, and accuracy wants both columns.

✦

From Where He Did Not Expect

Provision did not fall on the saddlers’ lane the way rain falls in stories, all at once and from straight above. It moved sideways, hand to hand, wearing faces and excuses, so that afterwards you could explain every part of it and still not be able to explain the whole. That is how it usually moves. The explanation is true, and it is not the truth.

It came first wearing the muhtasib’s dusty turban. In the worst month, the market inspector caught two bakers with chalk in their white flour and a third with weighted scales, and the lane watched them ride backwards through the market with their shame cried out before them. The same week, the amir’s storehouse opened a measure of grain for public bread, as rulers do when the mosques grow loud with the poor, and the inspector was told to choose ovens that could be trusted with other people’s flour. He did not deliberate long. “The one near the southern gate,” he said. “The one whose scales I have never had to touch. His bread is dark and his weights are clean.”

So the official grain came to Yunus’s oven, sacks of it on grey donkeys, and with it came a wage, paid in flour, which is the only coin a famine respects. The oven that had been banked to embers now burned from the dawn prayer to the night one, and the lane rediscovered a smell it had been mourning. Work, Yunus thought, standing in the roar of his own oven with his shirt soaked through in the middle of winter. No angel had handed him a purse. He had been handed labour, which is provision wearing an apron.

Heat itself became bread. The neighbours came shy at first, then not, to set their pots of beans in the falling warmth of the oven overnight, the old custom, and to sit against the warm wall with their children in the crook of their arms. The oven fed people it never fed, if you understand the arithmetic. A woman whose house has one warm wall in it does not spend her last strength shivering, and strength unspent is a meal nobody cooked.

Then it came wearing a letter. The widow Umm Hasan, it turned out, had not been asleep all those months ago. She had heard the basket and opened her shutter a finger’s width, and had seen exactly what the darkness was doing in her lane, and had kept the secret the way the poor keep the secrets of the generous, as treasure. She wrote to her son Salim, a clerk of weights in Alexandria, a young man of small salary and exact conscience. In spring a boat brought a sack of Rumi wheat with a letter tied to its neck: for the oven that does not sleep, from a son whose mother did not die this winter.

It arrived, and Safiyya would tell this part for the rest of her life, in the very week the amir’s grain was ending, when her first cord had grown heavy with knots and her nerve had begun to fray again. She stood in the doorway with the sack at her feet and the letter in her hand, and could not speak. Umm Hasan, watching from her own door, laughed until she coughed, and then told her everything: the shutter, the finger’s width, the basket, the man who thought the dark kept his secrets. “You married a thief,” said the widow. “I saw him with these eyes, stealing bread into houses.”

And behind all of it, farther away than any of them could see, it came wearing weather. That summer, in the far south past the cataracts, in highlands none of them would ever visit, rain fell. It fell for weeks on mountains they did not know the names of, and the water did what water has been commanded to do since the beginning: it gathered, it obeyed its slopes, it came down a thousand miles to find the fields of Egypt. The criers went up from the Nilometer, and this time their voices climbed, cubit by cubit, and the city wept at a number.

That night Safiyya sat late over her two cords, the leaving cord and the arriving cord, trying to make the winter balance the way an honest ledger should. The knots would not cooperate. Flour had left by measures; what had come back had come as a wage, a wall, a wheat sack, a waived fee, a rain a thousand miles away, none of it in units her fingers could tie. She laughed once, short, at her own hands, and said, “It will not balance. I cannot make it balance.” “It never balances,” said Yunus from the dark. “It overflows on one side. I stopped trying to catch it in a cup years ago. I just bake.”

The Measure

The next year, when the flood came full and the drums beat at the cutting of the canal, and boys leapt into the brown water with their clothes on, Yunus stood at the back of the crowd like a man at a reunion. The loaves at his window had grown back to their old roundness, the way faces on the lane had. Dawud was suddenly tall, all elbows, and worked the peel beside his father. Ruqayya had appointed herself keeper of the sour mother and fed it every night with the seriousness of a clerk, folding today into tomorrow the way it had been done for sixty years.

On the first Friday after the flood, the khatib, the preacher, rose in the mosque near the southern gate and recited into the hush the verses of the chapter of divorce, of all places, the verses every poor man in Egypt carried like a coin sewn into a coat: whoever is mindful of Allah, He makes for him a way out, and provides for him from where he does not expect. And whoever leans his whole weight on Allah, He is enough for him. Yunus had heard the verses all his life. This time they read like his own household accounts, entered in a hand finer than Safiyya’s.

From where he does not expect. He sat with his rough hands open on his knees and walked back through the winter, door by door. Not one sack of it had come from where he had planned. He had planned only the giving away, the emptying side of the ledger. The filling had come sideways, through an inspector’s scales, a widow’s shutter, a clerk’s conscience, a wall’s warmth, and a rain in the far south that had been decreed before any of them was born. He had spent his whole life calling Allah the Provider with his tongue, one Name in a memorized string. It is a different thing to find the Name’s fingerprints on your own flour bin.

He never grew rich, in case you are waiting for that. The oven near the southern gate stayed small, and Yunus died an old man with flour in the creases of his hands and no property to divide except a jar of living dough, which Ruqayya took, and a copper basket, which Dawud took, and a custom, which the lane took and kept for a hundred years without remembering whose it was: in hard months, bread appeared on doorsteps before the dawn prayer, and no one saw it come, and everyone knew better than to look.

Safiyya outlived him, still accurate. She hung the two cords on a nail by the door where the winter light caught them, the heavy one and the light one, and when her grandchildren asked she would run her thumb along the knots like a woman reading. “This one is everything the house gave away in the famine year,” she would say. “And this one is everything that came. I tied them both myself, so I can tell you honestly which string ran out first.” Then she would hang them back on the nail, leaving the question standing in the room, doing its quiet work.

The oven cooled every night of its life. The Providing never did.

وَمَن يَتَّقِ اللَّهَ يَجْعَل لَّهُ مَخْرَجًا ﴿٢﴾ وَيَرْزُقْهُ مِنْ حَيْثُ لَا يَحْتَسِبُ ۚ وَمَن يَتَوَكَّلْ عَلَى اللَّهِ فَهُوَ حَسْبُهُ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ بَالِغُ أَمْرِهِ ۚ قَدْ جَعَلَ اللَّهُ لِكُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدْرًا ﴿٣﴾

And whoever is mindful of Allah, He makes for him a way out, and provides for him from where he does not expect. And whoever relies upon Allah, He is enough for him. Indeed, Allah accomplishes His purpose. Allah has set a measure for every thing.
