Al-Ghaffar · The Ever-Forgiving

الغَفَّار

The Ink That Would Not Dry

The paper market of Cordoba, al-Andalus, the 900s

Before you begin

For whoever is carrying a wrong that no one ever caught: this one ends forgiven, not erased.

People forgive like doors. Allah forgives like dawn.

The Man Who Read with His Hands

In the years when Cordoba was the lamp of the West, when men said her streets could be walked by lamplight from one end to the other and her books outweighed her treasury, there was a lane behind the great mosque where the warraqin kept their stalls: the paper men, the copyists, the sellers of written things. The lane smelled of gall ink, cedar shavings, and the cold water smell of new paper. Buyers came from Fez and Qayrawan and the far towns of the frontier carrying silver, and left carrying weightless fortunes: books wrapped in cloth like infants.

The best stall in the lane belonged to Abu Yahya the warraq, and Abu Yahya was blind. He had given his eyes to the page over forty years, letter by letter, by lamplight, in the fine small hand of al-Andalus, and when the last light went out of them he did not leave the trade; he simply began to read with his hands. Merchants tested him for sport. They would lay an unnamed volume across his palms, and he would weigh it, smell the leather, press one leaf between finger and thumb, listen to it turn, and name the book, the paper, the decade, and once even the copyist. He was rarely wrong and never boastful about it.

Ziyad came to him at twelve, a rope maker’s orphan with quick eyes and a quicker hand, first to sweep, then to stir the ink, then to cut the reed pens, and at last to keep the daftar, the ledger, in which every sale and debt of the stall was written. By seventeen he was the old man’s eyes. Every evening they sat in the courtyard while Ziyad read the day’s lines aloud, and the master listened with his fingers resting on the edge of the page, lightly, the way a man warms his hands at a fire he cannot see.

Ink is a trust, boy, the master told him in those years. A merchant’s lie dies with the bargain, but a written lie stands up and testifies for a hundred years. We are people of the pen. When we lie, we lie forever. He said it without heat, the way he said everything, and Ziyad wrote it on his heart the way boys do: deeply, and then over it, other things.

The craft filled their days. Oak galls from the frontier hills, crushed and steeped with green vitriol and gum until the ink came up black with a shine in it. Reeds cut at the merciful angle. The famous red leather of Cordoba, supple as cloth, for the bindings of those who could pay. Gold for the unwans, the chapter roses of great Qur’ans. And in the east of the city, it was said in the suq with pride, a whole street of women earned their bread copying Qur’ans in hands so fair that judges wept over the pages. It was a city drunk on written things, and the two of them lived at the bottom of its cup.

As for Ziyad’s religion in his nineteenth year, it was a coat worn because it hung by the door. He prayed the way he swept the shop: because it was his to do. He fasted with the city and feasted with the city. His faith had never yet been asked a hard question, and like an unproved rope, it looked much the same as a strong one.

Dice Under the Arches

Below the city the old bridge crossed the great river, al-Wadi al-Kabir, and under its arches at night there was another market, one with no ledgers at all. Mule drivers, rivermen, soldiers off their watch, and the dice going down on leather in the lamplight with a small sound like rain beginning. A small, promising sound. Ziyad stood one evening at the edge of the light, only to watch, which is how every long fall begins: at an edge, only watching.

The dice were courteous to him at first; it is how they introduce themselves. He watched, then he held another man’s stake, then he played his own, and won, and laughed, and came back. Nard, the old game, forbidden and everywhere. For a month the arches ate his evenings and paid him back in coppers and excitement. Then in one week of black luck the debt stood at eight dinars, owed to a soft voiced man whose friends had heavy hands, and the week he was given to find it had six days left, then five.

Eight dinars was half a year of his wage. He thought of telling the master and could not carry the thought to the end; the old face turned toward him, blind and knowing, was worse than the heavy hands. He thought of his dead father, who had woven rope, honest strand over honest strand, and died without one dirham owed to any man. And he prayed. That week he prayed more than he had prayed in the whole year before it: the prayer of a man asking Allah to pull him out of a hole while he goes on digging it, which is a real prayer, and heard, though not always answered in the shape requested.

The cash box was hopeless; the master counted it each evening by touch, and his fingers were a court of law. But the daftar had no fingers on it except Ziyad’s own. On the day the physician from Toledo came for the great herbal, a fine book with three months of a good copyist’s life in it, the price was thirty dinars, counted warm into Ziyad’s palm. He wrapped the book, salaamed the buyer to the door, sat down, and in his fair, praised hand wrote twenty-two. The lie took less ink than his name.

That evening in the courtyard he read the day’s lines aloud, the false one among them, and his voice did not shake. When he finished, the old man smiled toward the sound of him. Allah has put barakah, blessing, in this shop since the day you came to it, he said. Ziyad looked at the ledger and said nothing at all. He had just learned how large a thing it is to be seen by no one, and could not yet say the rest of it: by no one, except One.

He paid the debt under the arches the next night, coin by coin into the soft palm. Then he walked to the middle of the old bridge and threw the dice as far out over the black water as his arm could send them. He never gambled again; that repentance took one throw. The other repentance took nineteen years.

The Line That Stayed Wet

At first he told himself the story every thief tells: it was a loan. He would return it quietly, he would slip the coins back into the count, eight dinars was nothing to a stall that handled thirty in an afternoon. But there was never a safe day, and then there was never a safe month, and after enough time the lie needed feeding: he began to give eight dirhams to the beggars at the mosque door on Fridays, always eight, as if mercy could be bribed with its own coin. The beggars blessed him. The blessings landed like small stones.

The years were good to him in every visible way, which is its own strange punishment. His hand grew famous in the lane; buyers began to ask for the young warraq by name. The master aged, and leaned, and gave him more of the stall each season. One evening the old man said: when I am gone this shop is yours, as it has been in truth for years; what is mine is yours to keep, as you have kept everything else. The sentence went into Ziyad like a splinter pushed deeper, to a place the fingers could not follow.

It was the prayers that changed first. In sujud, forehead on the reed mat, at the moment of the whole body’s honesty, the ledger line would rise behind his closed eyes, written in light: twenty-two. He began to stand in the last row of the mosque, near the door, like a guest unsure of his welcome. In Ramadan the city glowed and he moved through it hollow, breaking his fast each evening on dates and the taste of iron. He did not leave the prayer. Something in him understood that if he left it, the line would have taken everything. He prayed carrying the line, year after year, the way a man works with a stone in his shoe.

He married Maryam, a silk weaver’s daughter with steady eyes, and loved her, and was kind. She noticed what wives notice. You sleep like a man listening for knocking, she told him once, in the dark. He almost told her that night, and did not, and the almost lay between them like a drawn line no one stepped over.

The master grew smaller each winter, and his praise grew worse. When I stand before Allah, he said one evening, feeling for his cup, I will tell Him: I had a son by ink if not by blood, and in all his years he never gave me one false line. Ziyad made an excuse about closing the shutters, went out, and was sick against the cold stone of the mosque wall like a boy who cannot hold his wine.

It broke him open on a Friday. The khatib that week recited from Surah az-Zumar: say, O My servants who have been excessive against their own souls, do not despair of the mercy of Allah; surely Allah forgives sins, all of them together. All of them, the khatib repeated, and the word went through the pillared dark like a wind through wheat. Ziyad stood with his back against a cool column and wept into his sleeve, silently, the way grown men weep in public: with the body held still and the water simply leaving.

Afterward he found the old faqih, the jurist who sat between two columns after the prayer answering whoever came. He asked for a friend, the way everyone asks. My friend took money by deceit from one who trusts him, years ago, and was never discovered, and cannot sleep.

The faqih looked at him the mild way old scholars look, which is a way that has seen a thousand friends. Tell your friend two things, he said. The first: Allah’s own right is forgiven at a turn of the heart, for He is Al-Ghaffar, the Ever-Forgiving, and the Prophet ﷺ promised that the one who repents from a sin is as one who has no sin at all. The second: what was taken from a man must walk back to that man. Repentance has feet, my son. It cannot be performed lying down. And there is no road to the first door except through the second. Then he added, kindly: may Allah make the walking easy for your friend.

Eight Dinars, Weighed

He did not sleep that night, and in the morning he weighed out twenty dinars: eight for the taking, and twelve because nineteen years had passed and he did not know the fiqh of shame, only its arithmetic. He walked the lane he had walked all his life, past doors he had never once noticed were doors, every one of them standing at some angle between open and shut, and came to the stall, and found the master alone in the courtyard with the morning sun on his white head.

He put the coins down on the low table so that they rang, and knelt, and said it plainly, because he had learned from ledgers that the plain entry is the only honest one. Abu Yahya. In the year the Toledo physician bought the great herbal, he paid thirty dinars. I wrote twenty-two. I took eight, for a dice debt, and you praised me that evening, and I have eaten that praise for nineteen years, and it has eaten me. Here is the money with its years. Do with me what you will.

The silence had weight and edges. The old hands came forward and found the coins and counted them, twice, the fingers unhurried, the face unreadable as all blind faces are and his more than most. Eight, he said at last, and twelve that are not mine. Take back the twelve, or you will make a thief of me in my old age too. Then the tears came down the old face, simply, without sound, and Ziyad, who had prepared himself for anger, found he had prepared for the wrong thing entirely.

Eight dinars I would have put in your hand that same night, had you asked, the master said. You knew that. That is the wound, Ziyad. You did not steal silver from me; silver cannot be stolen from a dying man, it was never his. You took the thing I told Allah about you. You let me thank Him for a lie. For nineteen years, whenever I boasted of you, you stood beside me and held the ledger.

He stopped, and gathered himself the way the very old gather themselves, from far away. And still, he said. And still. I forgive you for His sake, here, now, entirely as far as my saying goes. I will not carry this to His door, I leave it in this courtyard. But my heart is old and blind like the rest of me, boy. It must learn the shop again by touch. Give it time, and do not hate it for walking slowly. It walks slowly everywhere now.

So the pardon was given in a morning, and the healing was not, for they are different medicines and are sold in different markets. The neighbor’s boy began to come on some evenings after that, and read the ledger aloud in his high unbroken voice, and Ziyad would stand in the doorway holding a full inkwell with nowhere particular to set it down. The master still called him son. The master still walked to Friday prayer with a hand on Ziyad’s shoulder. But he was no longer the old man’s eyes; he was a man the old man loved, and checked. Forgiveness, Ziyad learned, is not restoration. The pardon was real. The praise never came back.

Warmth returned the way spring returns to a pruned tree: truly, but along different branches. They broke bread. They laughed sometimes, at the old jokes, which are the ones that survive. And between them, always, at the bottom of everything, lay one line in dried ink that both of them had stopped mentioning and neither of them had forgotten, because ink is a trust, and when we lie, we lie forever, and what the mercy of men can do with forever is soften it, not shorten it.

The Half-Open Door

The master’s last illness came three winters later, and Ziyad tended him the way a man tends his own repentance: completely. He carried him to the courtyard for the thin sun, trimmed his nails, warmed the water for his wudu and guided the old hands through it. In the afternoons he read to him, and the master asked most often for Surah Yusuf, and always, at the place where Yusuf says to the brothers who sold him: no blame upon you today, Allah forgives you, the old man would lift one finger from the blanket, as if to say: there, that is how it is done, and not one of us can do it.

One evening he said: take a pen. There is no other hand in Cordoba I would have hold it. Write my will. And Ziyad understood what was being handed back to him, because the wound had been made with ink, and now the ink was being returned. He wrote: the stall and its stock to Ziyad the warraq. A third of the coin to the poor of the paper lane, and let it be given quietly. And write this last, said the master: I die owed nothing by any man. Ziyad’s pen stopped over the page. Write it, the old man said gently. It is true. The account was settled in full. Only the receipt was slow.

Then, because dying men spend their last coins honestly, he said: now forgive me also, Ziyad. I said the words of pardon in one evening, and my heart came along years behind them, like an old servant on the stairs. I wanted to forgive you the way the dawn does it: all at once, over everything, asking nothing. I could only do it the way a door does: a little wider each year, and never quite to the wall. A man is only a man. The rest of the opening, take from Allah. He has it. He has all of it, and He gives it away entire, which no son of Adam has ever managed.

He died before the next Friday, with the fast of that day written in his book, and the market of written things closed its shutters for him as it had never closed for any prince. They prayed the janazah in the great mosque, rows of ink stained men, and buried him before Dhuhr. At the graveside, with the first earth in his hand, the sentence finished itself in Ziyad quietly, like a line drying: people forgive like doors. Allah forgives like dawn.

He kept the old daftar all his life. On the day of his confession he had done the only thing a warraq may do with a false line, for a page must never be torn out; the page is a witness. Under the lie he had written the true sum, and the date the money came home, and signed it. Ink cannot be unwritten. It can only be answered.

Years later he would open that page for his own apprentice, a sharp quick orphan with a fair hand, and lay his finger on the two lines and say: read them both. The first cost me nineteen years. The second cost eight dinars and one morning. The deed was heavy, boy, but it was the delay that nearly killed me. Sin is a debt, and shame is its interest, and there is only One in whose books the interest is always cancelled and never once collected.

As an old man he walked to the mosque at Fajr under the last stars, ink in the creases of his hands that no washing would ever take out. He liked to arrive early and watch the east. First the black went grey, then the grey went pale, and then, all at once, over everything, without effort and without partiality, the light came up the sky and the night was simply covered, entire, as if it had never been, and no one on earth could point to where the darkness had gone. He stood in the lane and watched it happen, every morning, an old man warming himself at a Name.

Of the two forgivenesses he had needed in his life, one had come slowly, human, half a door, and he was grateful for it to his last hour. The other had been waiting behind the hills the whole time: Al-Ghaffar, the Ever-Forgiving, who covers, and covers, and covers again, and is not wearied, and does not remind.

قُلْ يَا عِبَادِيَ الَّذِينَ أَسْرَفُوا عَلَىٰ أَنفُسِهِمْ لَا تَقْنَطُوا مِن رَّحْمَةِ اللَّهِ ۚ إِنَّ اللَّهَ يَغْفِرُ الذُّنُوبَ جَمِيعًا ۚ إِنَّهُ هُوَ الْغَفُورُ الرَّحِيمُ

Say: O My servants who have been excessive against their own souls, do not despair of the mercy of Allah. Surely Allah forgives sins, all of them together. Surely He is the Ever-Forgiving, the Most Merciful.
